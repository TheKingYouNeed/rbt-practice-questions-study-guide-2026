# RBT scenario triage checklist for practice questions

Scenario-based RBT practice questions often include several answers that sound
reasonable. The difficulty is not always recalling a definition. It is deciding
which detail matters, what the question is asking, and which action fits the
RBT's role.

This page offers a six-step triage checklist for reviewing original practice
questions. It is a study framework, not clinical direction. In actual services,
follow the written plan, employer procedures, training, and supervision.

## The six-step triage

### 1. Separate facts from interpretation

Underline only what the scenario directly states. Look for:

- what happened before and after the behavior;
- what was observed or measured;
- who gave an instruction;
- whether a procedure is already part of a written plan;
- whether the question describes an urgent safety issue; and
- the order in which events occurred.

Do not add an assumed diagnosis, function, preference, or intention. A
distractor may sound sophisticated because it adds an explanation that the
scenario never established.

**Review prompt:** What do I know from the question, and what am I merely
assuming?

### 2. Identify the task in the final sentence

The last sentence usually asks for one specific kind of decision. Common task
types include:

- identify a measurement or graphing concept;
- recognize an assessment or teaching procedure;
- select the next step in an established sequence;
- choose objective documentation;
- identify what should be reported; or
- recognize an ethics or role-boundary issue.

Restate the task in plain language before comparing the options. If the
question asks what the RBT should do **first**, an answer that might be useful
later can still be incorrect.

**Review prompt:** Am I answering the actual request, or reacting to the most
memorable detail?

### 3. Mark the role boundary

Ask which parts of the situation belong to implementation and which require a
supervisor's clinical judgment. An RBT may collect data, implement trained
procedures, document objectively, and communicate relevant information.
Designing or independently changing an intervention is different.

This check does not mean "contact the supervisor" is automatically correct.
The scenario may describe a routine task already covered by the plan and
training. Use the facts in the question rather than selecting a general phrase
that sounds safest.

**Review prompt:** Does this option implement an established direction, or
create a new clinical decision?

### 4. Find the controlling source

The strongest answer should align with the source that governs the described
action:

- the written behavior plan;
- the specified data-collection procedure;
- the instructions and training already provided;
- the RBT Ethics Code; or
- the supervisor and employer communication process.

If an option contradicts the written plan or asks the RBT to improvise beyond
training, eliminate it unless the scenario explicitly describes an immediate
safety response.

**Review prompt:** What plan, procedure, or professional duty controls this
choice?

### 5. Eliminate by reason, not by familiarity

For every rejected option, name one concrete weakness. Useful labels include:

- adds a fact that is not given;
- changes the plan independently;
- answers a different question;
- occurs in the wrong sequence;
- uses subjective language;
- ignores a relevant safety or privacy duty; or
- falls outside the role described.

This prevents a familiar phrase from winning simply because you remember
seeing it in another question. It also makes distractor rationales more useful:
you can compare the publisher's explanation with your own elimination reason.

**Review prompt:** Can I explain why each alternative is weaker?

### 6. Choose and justify in one sentence

Complete this sentence before checking the rationale:

> The best answer is ___ because the scenario states ___, and that requires
> ___.

A complete sentence exposes weak reasoning. If the explanation only repeats
the answer choice, return to the relevant concept or official task statement.

After reviewing the rationale, revise the sentence if necessary. Save missed
and low-confidence questions for a later retry rather than repeating them
immediately from memory.

## Four traps the checklist catches

### The unearned conclusion

The answer assumes a function, preference, or clinical explanation that was not
established. Return to the observable facts and the data described.

### The correct concept in the wrong order

The option names a valid action but skips a step. Pay close attention to words
such as *first*, *next*, *before*, and *after*.

### The helpful-sounding plan change

The option proposes a new prompt, reinforcer, target, or intervention without
the plan or supervision described in the scenario. Check the role boundary.

### The broad ethics slogan

The option uses a generally positive phrase but does not address the specific
privacy, competence, documentation, communication, or boundary issue in the
question. Identify the duty before choosing.

## A printable one-line checklist

Write this at the top of a practice page:

**FACTS -> TASK -> ROLE -> SOURCE -> ELIMINATE -> JUSTIFY**

Then use six short notes:

1. Facts only:
2. What is asked:
3. RBT role boundary:
4. Controlling plan, procedure, or duty:
5. Why the distractors fail:
6. One-sentence justification:

The notes do not need to be long. Their purpose is to make the decision process
visible so you can diagnose whether a miss came from content knowledge,
reading, sequence, role confusion, or distractor selection.

## A 20-minute practice cycle

Use the checklist with a small set of original RBT practice questions:

1. Spend 10 minutes answering without notes.
2. Mark every low-confidence answer, including correct guesses.
3. Spend 7 minutes writing one-sentence justifications and reviewing
   rationales.
4. Spend 3 minutes choosing one concept for targeted review.

On the next day, answer new questions involving that concept. Later in the
week, return to the missed items after the original wording is less familiar.
